A history in writing

The Talking Leaves

Literacy gave the Cherokee standing to argue, not the power to decide. The standard for “civilization” was never theirs to set.

SEQUOYAH & THE CHEROKEE NATION1821 — TODAY

Nobody Believed Him

Ayoka learned her father’s characters quickly. Each one stood for a syllable in the Cherokee language, and when he brought her before Cherokee leaders in the summer of 1821, nobody believed what he was about to claim.

He said he had found a way to write down the Cherokee language. They were not impressed. To them, a man who knew no English had no business inventing a script, and some in the room suspected he was practising something closer to witchcraft than scholarship.

So he and Ayoka were separated and asked to prove it. Witnesses carried the messages back and forth, watching for a trick. They never caught one.

She read what he wrote. He read what she wrote.

Sequoyah called words on paper talking leaves.

Within a decade the Cherokee Nation was writing its own laws and printing its own newspaper. But why did a people who had always done without writing take to it so quickly?

A Nation That Could Not Read the Fine Print

When Sequoyah began, Cherokee towns stretched across what is now Georgia, Tennessee and Alabama. They had governed themselves for generations without a widely used writing system of their own. That mattered more as white Americans moved in and land claims came to be settled on paper.

Most of what they knew travelled by voice, from the terms of the last agreement to the boundaries of the last hunt, and among themselves that had always been enough. A written document worked differently. It could travel farther than a voice and stay fixed where it landed. To the officials deciding the Cherokee’s fate, that made it worth more than anything a person merely remembered.

Sequoyah understood that gap. He had served in Andrew Jackson’s army against the Creeks in 1814. What stayed with him was the sight of soldiers sending messages across ground they had never walked, by scratching on paper.

He tried to do the same thing. He began with pictures, one symbol for every word, and gave up when he could no longer keep track of them. Then he stopped asking how to draw the Cherokee language and started asking how it sounded.

He Took Their Shapes and Refused Their Sounds

Sequoyah did not invent his characters out of thin air. He took the shapes off a page of English.

He went looking for a Bible or a spelling book to study the letterforms, unable to read a word of what he was looking at. He copied the shapes and gave them Cherokee values instead. His most famous character, Ꭰ, looks exactly like a Latin D. In Cherokee it is read “a.” The same trick recurs throughout the set of characters.

The usual version of this story treats him as a genius who could not read. Recent scholarship, most thoroughly by Ellen Cushman, reads the syllabary instead as a Cherokee technology that helped shape Cherokee identity and strengthen the Nation’s political standing. “He could not read” is true, and it is also the framing that keeps the story safely inside an alphabetic worldview, where the only way to invent writing is to almost copy someone else’s. Whatever scholars later made of it, the syllabary worked.

Within a few years it had spread across the Nation, and by 1827 the Cherokee had a constitution of their own, with three branches and a two-house legislature, modelled on the United States Constitution.

Next came a printing press. The script had no type, so the Nation sent a young Cherokee named Elias Boudinot north to raise money, and the missionary Samuel Worcester arranged for a font to be cast in Boston.

On February 21, 1828, the first issue of the Cherokee Phoenix came off that press, a weekly in Cherokee and English, edited by Boudinot and published by a nation Georgia insisted did not exist.

They Won the Case Nobody Enforced

Georgia had wanted the Cherokee gone since 1802, when it traded away its western land claims to Washington for a federal promise to clear Native people out of the state. By the 1820s its politicians were demanding that Washington keep that promise, and when gold turned up near Dahlonega the state stopped waiting. It extended its laws over the Nation, surveyed Cherokee land, and began handing it out by lottery to white residents. In 1830 Congress passed the Indian Removal Act, giving the whole scheme federal backing.

The Cherokee answered the way a literate nation does. They sued and lost the first case in 1831 on jurisdiction. A year later, in Worcester v. Georgia, the Supreme Court ruled that Georgia’s laws had no force on Cherokee land. Jackson made no move to enforce the ruling, and Georgia felt no need to withdraw its laws. The famous line attributed to him, “John Marshall has made his decision; now let him enforce it,” is probably something he never said, but it captures what he did: nothing.

A verdict on paper is still only paper if nobody with power agrees to read it.

The ruling changed nothing on the ground, and some Cherokee began to wonder if anything would. In December 1835, about twenty of them, Boudinot among them, signed the Treaty of New Echota, trading every acre east of the Mississippi for land in the west and five million dollars. None had been authorized to speak for the Nation, which had already made any such cession punishable by death. The U.S. Senate approved the treaty 31-15. Four years later, three of the Cherokee men who had signed it were killed by their own people, for signing.

Their signatures never made the treaty legitimate. The United States did not need them to.

The army came in 1838. Roughly sixteen thousand Cherokee were driven into stockades, held through a summer and marched west. At least four thousand died. It is called the Trail of Tears.

The easy lesson is that writing did not stop removal. The harder one is that literacy gave the Cherokee new ways to organize, argue and resist; it gave them a voice, not a vote, in what came next.

The Standard Moved Every Time They Reached It

White Americans came to call the Cherokee, Choctaw, Chickasaw, Creek and Seminole the “Five Civilized Tribes,” a label that sounded like praise but was really a shortlist assembled by people who had already decided what civilization looked like. In 1893, Congress used it to name the commission created to negotiate land allotment agreements that would ultimately dismantle their tribal governments.

Cherokee leaders had done nearly everything those standards called for: a written constitution, representative government, schools, settled farms. Recognition stayed conditional, and the terms kept changing. For decades U.S. officials had urged the Cherokee to give up hunting and farm like white American settlers. Many did, and the pressure to leave only grew. The standard kept moving because the conflict was never really about literacy. It was about land, and who had the power to decide.

Native leaders answered the colonizers’ standard in the colonizers’ own language, filing letters, petitions and memorials in English. It was not enough to stop removal.

The Nation’s most fluent writers lost the argument, and one of them signed the treaty that took the land. Boudinot had helped raise the money for the Phoenix and edited it until 1832, when he decided removal could not be stopped and wanted the paper to debate it. Chief John Ross forbade the Phoenix to print a word in favor of removal, and Boudinot resigned. The Nation’s own newspaper would not carry the argument he wanted to make, so he made it elsewhere, and in the end he made it with his signature.

Writing serves whoever picks it up. It carried an argument for staying and an argument for leaving, and it could not decide between them.

What the Paper Kept

The records survived. The Phoenix can still be read today in both its languages, and the constitution and court briefs survive beside it. That record kept Cherokee resistance visible in the archives and institutions that later told the story of removal. The Potawatomi, driven from Indiana in the same autumn of 1838 on a march their descendants call the Trail of Death, received less attention.

The language is another matter. In Tahlequah, Oklahoma, Cherokee Nation runs an immersion school where young children learn the syllabary as their way into a language most of their own grandparents no longer speak fluently. Researchers who have studied the program describe it less as a return to how Cherokee was once spoken than as an ongoing negotiation between two languages, carried out by children who move between them without much trouble. It is not the voice Ayoka read aloud in 1821. But it is not silence, either.

Writing did not stop the removal, and it has not saved the language. The United States could force the Cherokee west, but it could not write the last page. More than two hundred years after Ayoka read her father’s words aloud, children in Tahlequah are still reading Cherokee, in the characters he created.
